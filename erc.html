Authoritarian Threats to Scientific Knowledge

AutoKnow

How is science shaped by authoritarian rule? AutoKnow studies when governments attack scientists, whether political pressure alters core scientific norms such as openness and critical inquiry, and how authoritarian politics leaves traces in the content of research itself. The project is led by Tore Wig and hosted by the Department of Political Science, University of Oslo.

Principal Investigator Tore Wig Host University of Oslo Duration May 2026 – April 2031 Funding €2 million, ERC Grant No. 101231399

About the project

Science is often assumed to be insulated from politics. Yet across the world, universities and researchers operate under governments that range from open democracies to closed autocracies, and a growing number of democracies are sliding in an authoritarian direction. AutoKnow asks what this means for the knowledge science produces. Does it matter for the findings of social science whether the country producing them is authoritarian? When do regimes go after scientists, and which fields and topics are most exposed?

The project develops a new theory of science and authoritarian rule. It explains how scientific knowledge production is shaped by the difference between autocracies and democracies, by features of authoritarian regimes such as ideology, repression and institutions, and by authoritarian politics in backsliding democracies. The aim is both to document the scale of the problem and to identify what protects scientific progress against contemporary authoritarian pressures.

Research questions

  1. How does authoritarian rule, compared with democracy, affect the volume, quality and content of scientific research?
  2. When and why do authoritarian governments attack scientists and scientific institutions, and which regime features, such as ideology, repression and institutional design, make attacks more likely?
  3. Does political pressure change fundamental scientific norms, such as openness, critical inquiry and willingness to challenge established ideas, and can these changes be detected in published research?
  4. What happens to science in democracies that are backsliding, and what conditions protect scientific knowledge production against authoritarian pressure?

Approach

AutoKnow combines big data, artificial intelligence and modern statistical methods. Using large language models and other text-analysis tools, the project analyses millions of scientific publications from 1950 to 2025 to detect changes in research content, including political bias, censorship and a lack of novelty. Bibliometric measures are linked to historical data on regimes, repression and academic freedom to trace how political conditions shape what is studied, what is found and what is published.

This is combined with historical case studies and original data on attacks on scientists and scientific institutions. The team draws on expertise in comparative politics, statistics and informatics at the University of Oslo.

Join the project

AutoKnow will recruit two postdoctoral fellows and two doctoral research fellows over the course of the project. Positions are announced through the University of Oslo vacancies page. Candidates with a background in political science, computational social science, statistics or related fields, and an interest in authoritarian politics and the politics of science, are encouraged to get in touch with Tore Wig.
